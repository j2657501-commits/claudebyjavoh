A Honeybees are among the most valuable insects on Earth. B In the winter of 2006, beekeepers in the United States began reporting something strange. Worker bees were vanishing from their hives, leaving behind the queen, plenty of food and a few young bees. A Coral reefs cover less than one per cent of the ocean floor, yet they are home to around a quarter of all marine species. For this reason they are often called the rainforests of the sea. Over thousands of years these skeletons accumulate into enormous structures.

B Corals get their colour, and most of their food, from microscopic algae that live inside their tissue. The relationship benefits both sides: the algae receive shelter, and in return they supply the coral with energy made from sunlight. This is why reefs grow only in clear, shallow water where light can reach them.

C The partnership is fragile, however. When sea temperatures rise by just one or two degrees for several weeks, the stressed coral expels its algae and turns white, an event known as bleaching. A bleached coral is not dead, and it can recover if the water cools quickly. If the heat continues, though, it slowly starves.

D Reefs matter to people as well as to fish. They protect coastlines by breaking the force of waves, and hundreds of millions of people rely on them for food or for income from tourism. Scientists are now experimenting with growing heat-resistant corals in nurseries and planting them on damaged reefs. Such projects are promising, but most experts agree that they cannot succeed unless the warming of the oceans is slowed.

A Every autumn, billions of birds leave their breeding grounds and fly to warmer regions, returning in spring. Some of these journeys are astonishing. The Arctic tern travels from the Arctic to the Antarctic and back each year, a round trip of more than 70,000 kilometres, while the bar-tailed godwit can fly for over a week across the Pacific Ocean without landing once.

B How birds navigate over such distances puzzled naturalists for centuries. It is now clear that they use several methods at the same time. During the day many species use the position of the sun, adjusting for the time with an internal clock. At night they steer by the stars. C Birds can also detect the Earth's magnetic field, although exactly how they do so is still debated. One theory suggests that light-sensitive molecules in the eye allow a bird literally to see the field. Closer to home, birds switch to landmarks such as rivers, coastlines and mountain ranges, and some seabirds even appear to use smell.

D Migration is risky. Storms, hunger and predators kill many birds on the way, and human activity has added new dangers. Brightly lit cities confuse birds that fly at night, and the wetlands where they stop to rest and feed are disappearing. Protecting these stopover sites, researchers say, is as important as protecting the places where birds nest.

A Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's crust through which molten rock, ash and gas escape. B Eruptions can be catastrophic. In AD 79 Mount Vesuvius buried the Roman town of Pompeii under several metres of ash. D Today scientists can often tell when an eruption is approaching. They measure small earthquakes, changes in the shape of the mountain and the gases escaping from it. Prediction is still far from exact, but timely warnings have saved many thousands of lives in recent decades.

A Anyone who has spent a day at the seaside has seen the water creep up the beach and then retreat again. Along most coasts this happens twice a day, and the cause lies 384,000 kilometres away. The gravity of the Moon pulls on the Earth, and because water moves easily, the oceans bulge slightly towards the Moon. A second bulge forms on the opposite side of the planet. As the Earth rotates, a coastline passes through both bulges, producing two high tides and two low tides roughly every twenty-five hours.

B The Sun also influences the tides, though its effect is less than half that of the Moon because it is so much farther away. When the Sun, Earth and Moon are in a line, at full moon and new moon, their forces combine to create especially large tides called spring tides. The name has nothing to do with the season. When the Sun and Moon pull at right angles, the result is the weaker neap tide.

C The shape of the coast makes an enormous difference. In the open ocean the water rises by less than a metre, but in narrow bays it can be funnelled to great heights. In the Bay of Fundy in Canada the difference between high and low tide can exceed fifteen metres.

D Tides have long been useful to people. Fishermen and sailors plan their work around them, and tidal mills were grinding grain in Europe over a thousand years ago. Today engineers are building turbines that generate electricity from tidal currents, a source of energy that, unlike wind, is entirely predictable.

A We tend to think of memory as a kind of video recording that can be played back whenever we wish. Psychologists have shown that this picture is wrong. Remembering is an active process of reconstruction, and each time we recall an event we rebuild it, sometimes adding details that were never there.

B Researchers usually distinguish three stages. Sensory memory holds what we see and hear for a second or two. A small part of this passes into short-term memory, which can keep about seven items for less than half a minute unless we repeat them. Only information that is rehearsed, or that matters to us emotionally, is transferred to long-term memory, where it may last a lifetime.

C The German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus studied forgetting in the 1880s by memorising lists of meaningless syllables. He found that most forgetting happens very quickly, within the first day, and then slows down. He also discovered that reviewing material at increasing intervals, a technique now called spaced repetition, is far more effective than studying it many times in one session.

D Because memories are rebuilt, they can be distorted. In a well-known experiment by Elizabeth Loftus, people watched a film of a car accident. Such findings have made courts more cautious about relying on the testimony of eyewitnesses.
